\paragraph{In-context regression as a testbed.} \citet{garg2022} trained GPT-2-style transformers from scratch on prompts $(x_1, f(x_1), \dots, x_n, f(x_n), x_q)$ for simple function classes. For linear functions with isotropic Gaussian inputs in $d = 20$, the trained model matched the error of least squares on unseen functions. They also tested shifts at evaluation time: with a skewed input covariance (eigenvalues $\propto 1/i^2$ in a random basis), the model's error tracked least squares up to about ten examples and then plateaued, whereas noiseless least squares becomes exact once $n \ge d$. They trained with Adam (learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch 64, 500,000 steps) and a curriculum over $d$ and $n$. We take their model size, $d$, prompt length and batch size, but put the same $1/j^2$ eigenvalues into training, with the coordinate axes rather than a random basis as principal directions, and vary the optimizer.

\citet{akyurek2023} asked which learning algorithm a trained in-context learner implements by comparing its predictions with reference algorithms. They constructed transformers that implement gradient descent and closed-form ridge regression, and found that trained models matched different algorithms depending on depth and noise: in noiseless regression, one-layer models were closest to one step of gradient descent, two- to four-layer models to ridge regression and models with eight or more layers to least squares. \citet{bai2023} constructed transformers that implement least squares, ridge, Lasso, generalized linear models and gradient descent on two-layer networks in context, and showed that one transformer can choose a different algorithm for each prompt (``in-context algorithm selection''). A transformer can therefore represent many in-context algorithms, so which one a trained model runs depends on how it was trained. We borrow Akyürek et al.'s method of comparing predictions with reference estimators (Section~\ref{sec:measure}), with two families suited to anisotropic inputs: ridge and principal component regression.

\paragraph{Training decides which estimator emerges.} \citet{raventos2023} varied the number of distinct tasks seen in pretraining. Below a task-diversity threshold the transformer behaves like the Bayes estimator whose prior is uniform over the finite set of pretraining tasks and fails on unseen tasks; above it, it behaves like ridge regression and solves new tasks near-optimally. More weight decay lowered the threshold, while more layers or a larger embedding raised it, so a training choice moved which estimator emerged. \citet{olsson2022} linked in-context learning in language models to induction heads, which form early in training in a ``phase change'': a short window in which in-context learning improves sharply and the training loss shows a bump, a stretch of steeper decrease (their evidence is causal for small attention-only models and correlational for larger ones). \citet{singh2023} found, on synthetic few-shot classification sequences, that emergent in-context learning can be transient: it appears and then gives way to in-weights learning while the training loss keeps falling, and L2 regularization made it more persistent. \citet{raventos2023} and \citet{singh2023} trained with Adam, and Singh et al.\ list other optimizers as future work. The only theoretical analyses we know of how the optimizer changes the in-context estimator concern simplified Muon in one-layer linear attention \citep{dragutinovic2026,ma2026}, discussed below; Dragutinovi\'c et al.\ also compare SGD and Muon in a two-layer softmax transformer on an algorithmic cycle task, but we know of no comparison of optimizers on in-context regression in softmax transformers.

\paragraph{What attention computes on linear regression.} \citet{vonoswald2023} constructed linear self-attention weights whose forward pass is one step of gradient descent on the in-context squared loss, and found that a single layer trained (with Adam) on isotropic regression converges to this construction, while deeper stacks learn a curvature-corrected variant. For anisotropic inputs, \citet{ahn2023} proved that, for one linear-attention layer with $x \sim \mathcal N(0, \Sigma)$ and $w \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$, the global minimizer of the in-context loss implements one step of gradient descent preconditioned by approximately $\Sigma^{-1}$, with a finite-context correction $\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma/n$ that acts as a regularizer. \citet{zhang2023trained} proved that gradient flow from a suitable random initialization reaches this global minimum, and studied shifts at test time: the trained layer tolerates shifts of the task and, for long prompts, of the query, but not a rescaling of all test covariates; GPT-2 models of Garg et al.'s size were more robust to rescaled covariates but still fell short of least squares at the largest rescaling. \citet{mahankali2023} proved that with $x \sim \mathcal N(0,\Sigma)$ and $w \sim \mathcal N(0, \Sigma^{-1})$ every global minimizer of the one-layer loss is one $\Sigma^{-1}$-preconditioned gradient step, and concluded that the distribution of the inputs shapes the learned algorithm more than the conditional distribution of the targets given the inputs. More broadly, \citet{wang2025testtime} cast attention and state-space layers as associative memories that solve a key-to-value regression in the forward pass, with plain linear attention as one gradient step that ignores the covariance between key dimensions (it replaces the key Gram matrix by the identity). These results concern the end point of training or what an architecture can represent, whereas optimizers differ in the path. Our query-shift test (Section~\ref{sec:measure}) is close to the query shift of \citet{zhang2023trained}, but we read it at matched in-distribution error, during training, and compare optimizers.

\paragraph{The path: learning directions in order of variance.} \citet{zhang2025training} analysed the gradient-flow dynamics of multi-head linear attention trained from small initialization on regression with $x \sim \mathcal N(0, \Lambda)$. With merged key--query weights, the loss falls in a single abrupt drop. With separate rank-one keys and queries, training goes from saddle to saddle: the heads learn the principal directions of $\Lambda$ one at a time, in decreasing order of variance, with the time to learn direction $m$ scaling as $\lambda_m^{-2}$, so a model stopped on the $(m{+}1)$-th plateau approximately implements principal component regression on the first $m$ components. Both parametrizations end at the same solution, and the authors observe the same multiple loss drops in one-layer softmax attention with separate rank-one keys and queries. This variance-ordered, stage-like learning mirrors the exact solutions of \citet{saxe2014} for deep linear networks, in which input--output modes are learned in order of their strength. Our H1 asks whether this path survives in multi-layer softmax transformers with full-rank attention, and whether Muon avoids it.
